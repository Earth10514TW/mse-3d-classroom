Pull on a metal bar and it first stretches like a stiff spring, then gives way and flows, and finally thins at one spot and breaks. Each stage gives a number an engineer can look up: modulus, yield strength, tensile strength, elongation, hardness. This chapter measures those numbers, traces the flow to dislocations gliding on close-packed planes, and shows how rolling, alloying, fine grains and annealing move every one of them.

01

Shaping metals: hot work and cold work

% reduction = (t0 − t) / t0 · hot: T > Trecrystal

A metal part gets its shape in one of two ways. Casting pours molten metal into a mold of the final shape, which suits complex parts such as pistons. The others push solid metal around: rolling squeezes a slab between rolls into thinner, uniform sheet, extrusion forces a billet through a die hole to get a constant cross section, and forging presses or hammers a piece into shape, closing pores and making the metal more uniform.

Each forming process can run hot or cold, and the dividing line is the recrystallization temperature. Above it (hot rolling, hot extrusion at 0.5–0.75 Tm) the deformed grains are replaced by new ones as fast as they are squashed, so large reductions are easy, but the surface oxidizes. Below it (cold rolling, cold extrusion at about room temperature) the grains stay squashed. The metal becomes stronger and less ductile, so only a limited reduction is possible before it needs annealing (Unit 8).

Pick a process and watch it run. The animations are schematic cutaways. In rolling, volume is conserved, so a 50% thinner slab comes out twice as long.

KEY POINTS
  • Casting shapes liquid metal; rolling, extrusion and forging shape solid metal by plastic deformation.
  • Hot vs cold is set by the recrystallization temperature, not by "feels hot". Above it grains renew themselves; below it they stay deformed.
  • Cold work strengthens a metal and costs ductility, so large cold reductions need annealing in between.

02

Stress, strain and the elastic constants

σ = Eε · τ = Gγ · ν = −εlateral/εaxial · G = E / 2(1 + ν)

A deformation is elastic if the metal returns to its original size when the load is removed, and plastic if it does not. To compare specimens of different sizes, divide the force by the original area and the stretch by the original length:

σ = F / A0 ε = (ℓ − ℓ0) / ℓ0 = Δℓ / ℓ0

These are the engineering stress (Pa = N/m², or psi; 1 psi = 6.89 × 103 Pa) and engineering strain (m/m, or percent). In the elastic range they are proportional, σ = Eε, and E is Young’s modulus. A bar that stretches also thins; the ratio of the two strains is Poisson’s ratio, ν = −εlateral/εaxial, between 0.25 and 0.4 for metals.

Why not more than 0.5? Stretch an L × L × L cube by εx; the two sides shrink by εy = εz = −νεx. To first order the new volume is L3(1 + εx + εy + εz), so

ΔV / V = εx + εy + εz = εx(1 − 2ν)

An incompressible material keeps its volume, ΔV = 0, which forces ν = 0.5. Metals, at about 0.3, gain a little volume when pulled elastically.

A force parallel to a face gives a shear stress τ = S/A and a shear strain γ = a/h = tan θ, related by the shear modulus, τ = Gγ. For an isotropic material the three constants are not independent: G = E / 2(1 + ν). Pick a metal and check that rule against the measured G.

KEY POINTS
  • Engineering stress and strain use the original area and length: σ = F/A0, ε = Δℓ/ℓ0.
  • Elastic strains in metals are tiny, a few tenths of a percent at most. A stiffer metal (larger E) strains less under the same stress.
  • Stretching makes a bar thinner by ν times the axial strain, and for an isotropic metal G = E / 2(1 + ν), so G is roughly 0.38 E.
  • ΔV/V = ε(1 − 2ν). An incompressible material has ν = 0.5; with ν ≈ 0.3 a metal's volume grows slightly in tension.

03

The tensile test: reading a stress–strain curve

E · 0.2% offset yield · UTS · % elongation · σt = σ(1 + ε), εt = ln(1 + ε)

In a tensile test a standard specimen is pulled to failure. A load cell records the force and an extensometer the stretch, and the pair becomes one stress–strain curve. Four numbers come off it:

Modulus E
The slope of the first, straight part.
Yield strength σy
The stress at which significant plastic deformation begins. Since the curve bends gradually, the convention is the 0.2% offset yield strength: draw a line parallel to the elastic part starting at 0.2% strain and read where it cuts the curve.
Ultimate tensile strength (UTS)
The highest point of the engineering curve. Beyond it the specimen starts necking: deformation concentrates in one spot, the area there shrinks quickly, and the load falls until fracture.
Ductility
% elongation = (final − initial length)/initial length, measured by fitting the broken halves together; or % area reduction = (initial − final area)/initial area at the break. Porosity lowers ductility.

The engineering curve divides by the original area, so it droops after the UTS even though the metal in the neck keeps getting stronger. True stress and true strain use the instantaneous area and length. Until necking, when volume is conserved, σt = F/Ai = σ(ℓi/ℓ0) = σ(1 + ε) and εt = ∫dℓ/ℓ = ln(ℓi/ℓ0) = ln(1 + ε).

KEY POINTS
  • Yield strength is read with the 0.2% offset construction; UTS is the peak of the engineering curve; elongation is the strain at fracture.
  • Necking starts at the UTS. After that the engineering stress falls because the area shrinks, not because the metal weakens.
  • Tempering or cold rolling raises σy and UTS and lowers elongation: strength and ductility trade off.

04

Hardness: resistance to a permanent dent

HR = E − e · e = Δd / 0.002 mm

Hardness measures how well a material resists plastic deformation, by pressing a hard indenter into it. It is quick, nearly non-destructive, and tracks the yield strength. The tests differ in what they measure: Brinell (10 mm steel or carbide ball), Vickers (136° diamond pyramid, VHN = 1.854P/d1²) and Knoop (elongated diamond, KHN = 14.2P/l²) all measure the size of the dent; Rockwell measures its depth.

A Rockwell test first applies a minor load F0 = 10 kgf to seat the indenter and set the zero. The major load F1 is added, then removed again, and the indenter springs back a little. The permanent extra depth Δd is divided by 0.002 mm to give e = Δd / 0.002 mm, which is subtracted from a constant E:

HR = E − e E = 100 (diamond cone) or 130 (steel ball)

A deeper dent gives a smaller number, so a harder material reads higher. The slide example: a depth of 0.076 mm on scale A gives 100 − 38 = HRA 62, and 0.06 mm on scale B gives 130 − 30 = HRB 100.

05

Slip systems: where dislocations glide

FCC {111}⟨1̄10⟩ 4 × 3 = 12 · BCC {110}⟨1̄11⟩ 6 × 2 = 12 · HCP {0001}⟨112̄0⟩ 1 × 3 = 3

Polish a single crystal, pull it, and the surface develops fine steps, slip bands. Each step is where many dislocations (Chapter 4) have glided out along one crystal plane, the slip plane, shifting the block above it by whole atomic spacings in the slip direction.

Slip is not the only way to shear a crystal. In twinning a band of the crystal shears into the mirror image of the rest across a twin plane (also {111} in FCC). Slip moves one block by whole atomic spacings and leaves the lattice orientation unchanged; twinning moves each plane in the band by a fraction of a spacing, in proportion to its distance from the twin plane, and turns the lattice inside the band.

Slip prefers the densest planes and the densest directions: atoms on a close-packed plane are farthest from the next plane and need the smallest shear to slide past one another. A slip plane plus a slip direction in it is a slip system.

FCC (Cu, Al, Ni, Pb, Au, Ag, γFe)
Four {111} octahedral planes, each with three ⟨1̄10⟩ directions: 4 × 3 = 12 systems, on truly close-packed planes. FCC metals are ductile.
BCC (αFe, W, Mo, β brass)
No close-packed plane. Slip runs on the densest planes, six {110}, each with two ⟨1̄11⟩ directions: 6 × 2 = 12. αFe, Mo, W and Na can also slip on {211} (12 systems), and αFe and K on {321} (24).
HCP (Cd, Zn, Mg, Ti, Be)
With a high c/a ratio slip is confined to the single basal plane {0001} with three ⟨112̄0⟩ directions: 1 × 3 = 3 systems, so these metals are less ductile. Low-c/a Ti also slips on prism {101̄0} (3) planes, and both Ti and Mg can slip on pyramidal {101̄1} (6) planes.

5-1How a dislocation moves: edge vs screw

Whole planes of atoms do not slide past each other all at once: that would break every bond across the plane at the same time. Instead a dislocation glides across the crystal one row at a time, breaking and remaking only a single line of bonds at any instant.

Under the same shear stress τ (and Burgers vector b), an edge dislocation line lies perpendicular to b and glides parallel to τ and b, whereas a screw dislocation line lies parallel to b and glides perpendicular to both. Either way, once the dislocation exits the crystal, the top half has shifted by the exact same step of one atomic spacing.

KEY POINTS
  • Slip takes the densest plane and the densest direction in it, because those need the least shear.
  • Count slip systems as planes × directions per plane: FCC 12, BCC 12 on {110}, HCP basal only 3.
  • More independent slip systems, especially on truly close-packed planes, make a metal more ductile: FCC Cu and Al bend easily, HCP Zn and Mg crack sooner.

06

Schmid's law: how much of the pull reaches the slip plane

τr = σ cos λ cos Φ · slip when τr > CRSS

Pull a single-crystal rod with force F along its axis. A slip plane tilted at angle Φ (between its normal and the axis) has a larger area than the cross section, A1 = A0/cos Φ. Only part of the force lies along the slip direction, which makes angle λ with the axis: Fr = F cos λ. The shear stress that actually drives slip, the resolved shear stress, is

τr = Fr / A1 = (F cos λ) / (A0/cos Φ) = σ cos λ cos Φ

The crystal yields when τr reaches the critical resolved shear stress (CRSS), a property of the metal that depends on crystal structure, bonding, temperature and purity. The product cos λ cos Φ, the Schmid factor, is at most 0.5, at Φ = λ = 45°. When the slip plane is parallel or perpendicular to the pull the factor is zero and the crystal cannot slip on that system however hard you pull.

KEY POINTS
  • Only the shear stress on the slip plane, along the slip direction, moves dislocations: τr = σ cos λ cos Φ.
  • The Schmid factor cos λ cos Φ is at most 0.5. So a single crystal yields at σy = CRSS / (cos λ cos Φ), never below 2 × CRSS.
  • CRSS is tiny for pure metals (Cu 0.94 MPa) and climbs fast with impurities (Ti 13.7 → 90.1 MPa from 99.99% to 99.9% pure).

07

Strengthening: putting obstacles in the dislocations' way

Hall–Petch: σy = σ0 + k / √d

A metal yields when dislocations glide. Every strengthening mechanism makes that glide harder, by putting something in the way:

Strain (work) hardening
Plastic deformation multiplies dislocations, and they tangle and block each other. Dislocation density rises with the degree of deformation; cold-rolled 1018 steel is far stronger and far less ductile than annealed 1018.
Solid solution hardening
Solute atoms of a different size strain the lattice around them and pin passing dislocations.
Precipitation hardening
Fine particles of a second phase block glide. In Al–4%Cu aged at 130–190 °C, copper first clusters into GP zones 10–100 nm across, then metastable θ′ nucleates on dislocations, and finally equilibrium θ (CuAl2) forms.
Grain boundary hardening
A dislocation cannot cross into a differently oriented grain, so it piles up at the boundary. Smaller grains mean more boundaries and a higher yield strength, by the empirical Hall–Petch equation σy = σ0 + k / √d.
Nanotwin hardening
Twin boundaries block dislocations like grain boundaries do but barely scatter electrons. Inside a grain, twins a distance λ apart cut the free path of a dislocation, so when λ is much smaller than the grain size d, λ takes over the role of d in Hall–Petch. Copper with ~400 nm grains split by ~15 nm twins is both strong and a good conductor, promising for free-standing Cu interconnects (L. Lu, Science 304, 422, 2004).

7-1Solid solution hardening: dislocation–solute interaction

Above an edge dislocation (⊥) the extra half-plane squeezes the lattice into compression; below it the lattice is stretched open in tension. A larger solute atom carries its own surrounding compressive field: in (a) above the slip plane, compression meets compression and the two repel; in (b) below it, the larger atom fits into the tensile gap, relieves distortion, and attracts and pins the dislocation. Either way, the solute's local strain raises the stress needed for the dislocation to glide past, strengthening the metal.

7-2Too small: the inverse Hall–Petch effect

Hall–Petch cannot go on forever. Nanocrystalline metals are usually very strong (and hard) but not very ductile, and below a critical grain size, around 20 nm, the trend reverses. The grains are too small to hold dislocation pile-ups, and deformation switches from dislocation slip to grain boundary sliding, so the yield strength falls as d shrinks further. The maximum strength reachable by grain refinement sits at that crossover.

KEY POINTS
  • Five ways to strengthen a metal: strain hardening, solid solution, precipitates, grain boundaries, nanotwins. All of them hinder dislocation glide.
  • Hall–Petch: σy grows linearly with d−1/2. Halving the grain size does not double the strength; you need d four times smaller to double the k/√d term.
  • Cold work raises yield strength faster than tensile strength and lowers elongation, until the metal must be annealed.

08

Annealing: recovery, recrystallization, grain growth

process → microstructure → property

A cold-worked metal is strong but brittle, and full of stored energy in its dislocations. Heat it and it softens in three stages. Reheating to soften a cold-worked metal is called annealing.

Recovery
At a moderate temperature (Trecovery < Trecrystal) dislocations rearrange into lower-energy walls that divide each grain into subgrains, polygonization; with longer annealing neighbouring subgrains coalesce into larger ones. Internal residual stresses drop, but the grains keep their deformed shape and the strength barely changes.
Recrystallization
At a high enough temperature new strain-free grains nucleate and grow, either from a nucleus inside a deformed grain or by an old high-angle boundary migrating into a more deformed region. Strength falls sharply and ductility returns.
Grain growth
Once the deformed grains are consumed, the new grains keep growing to lower the total boundary area. Strength drifts down slowly.

8-1What sets the recrystallization temperature

Recrystallization is driven by the strain energy stored in the cold work, needs nucleation sites for the new grains, and needs thermal energy for long enough. So:

  • More prior deformation → lower Trecrystal and finer new grains.
  • Higher temperature → shorter time. Time and temperature trade off along an Arrhenius line, as in Chapter 5.
  • Larger initial grains → more deformation needed to recrystallize at the same temperature.
  • Higher purity → lower Trecrystal; solute atoms pin the moving boundaries.

Rule of thumb: in kelvin, Trecrystal is about 0.3–0.4 Tm for pure metals and about 0.5 Tm for alloys. The last column checks it against the table: 0.35–0.51, at or just above the pure-metal range, since these are commercial metals rather than very pure ones. That is why lead recrystallizes below room temperature and cannot be work-hardened at all at room temperature, while tungsten needs 1200 °C.

KEY POINTS
  • Recovery relieves residual stress by rearranging dislocations into walls; grain shape and strength barely change.
  • Recrystallization replaces the deformed grains with new strain-free ones; this is where strength drops and ductility returns.
  • Trecrystal is not a fixed property: more cold work, longer time and higher purity all lower it.

09

Superplasticity: stretching a metal 1000%

grains 5–10 µm · T > 0.5 Tm · strain rate 10−2–10−4 s−1

At high temperature and slow loading some alloys stretch 2000% without breaking. An annealed Ti alloy elongates 12% at room temperature, but up to 1170% at 870 °C and a loading rate of 1.3 × 10−4 s−1. Three conditions must hold at once:

  • very fine grains, 5–10 µm;
  • temperature above 0.5 Tm;
  • a slow strain rate, 0.01 to 0.0001 s−1.

What changes is the mechanism. There is very little dislocation activity. Instead the grains slide past one another along their boundaries, rotate, and are reshaped by diffusion along the boundaries, so they stay small and equiaxed while the specimen as a whole gets enormously longer. Fine grains provide plenty of boundary, heat makes boundary diffusion fast, and a slow rate gives it time to keep up. Industry uses this for blow forming car hoods.

Strain rate affects ductility well beyond superplasticity, often non-linearly, and the trend depends on the material, the temperature and the mechanism. Metals are generally more ductile at a low rate and less ductile at a high rate; polymers turn brittle when pulled fast; ceramics stay brittle either way, slightly more ductile when slow and slightly tougher when fast.
