An electron in an atom is not a tiny ball on a track. It is a wave that has to fit around the nucleus, and only waves that fit are allowed. That one rule gives atoms their sharp energy levels, the shapes of their electron clouds, the order of the periodic table, and finally the bonds that hold every material together. 01

What is inside an atom

Thomson's pudding vs Rutherford's nucleus

Around 1900 nobody knew what an atom looked like inside. Cathode-ray tubes had shown that electrons can be pulled out of any atom, so an atom must be made of a negative part (electrons) and a positive part. The question was how the positive part is arranged.

Thomson, 1897“raisin pudding”
The positive charge is spread evenly through the whole atom, with electrons sprinkled inside like raisins. Nothing in such an atom is dense enough to turn a fast α particle around.
Rutherford, 1911nuclear model
Almost all the mass and all the positive charge sit in a tiny nucleus at the center. The rest of the atom is nearly empty, occupied only by electrons.

Rutherford's group fired α particles (He2+) at a thin gold foil. Most went straight through. A few bounced back, which Rutherford compared to a shell fired at tissue paper coming back at you. An α particle is about 7300 times heavier than an electron, and a heavy projectile can only be turned back by something heavier than itself. So the positive charge and the mass had to be concentrated in a nucleus. Fire both models below and compare.

A SENSE OF SCALE

If the nucleus were a marble 1 cm across, the atom would be a stadium 100 m across, and the electrons would be somewhere in the stands.

KEY POINTS
  • An atom is about 100 pm (10−10 m) across. Its nucleus is about 0.01 pm, some 10 000 times smaller.
  • Proton and electron carry the same charge, ±1.602 × 10−19 C, but a proton is 1836 times heavier.
  • Back-scattered α particles prove that positive charge and mass are concentrated in a nucleus. The model still has a fatal flaw, shown in Unit 2.

02

Why the orbits have to be quantized

nλ = 2πr → L = mvr = nh / 2π

Rutherford's atom has electrons circling the nucleus like planets. Classical physics says this cannot last. An electron going around a circle is always accelerating toward the center, and an accelerating charge radiates light. The electron would lose energy, spiral inward and hit the nucleus in about 10−11 s. On the way down its orbit frequency rises smoothly, so the light it gives off should be a continuous smear of colors.

Real atoms do neither. They are stable, and hot hydrogen gives off light at a few sharp wavelengths only. Something forbids most orbits.

2-1The rule: the electron wave has to close on itself

In 1923 de Broglie proposed that an electron also behaves like a wave, with wavelength λ = h / mv (Unit 4). Bohr's allowed orbits then have a simple meaning. A wave running around a circle meets its own tail after one lap. If the circumference holds a whole number of wavelengths, crest lands on crest and the wave reinforces itself every lap: a stable standing wave. If not, each lap arrives shifted, and after many laps the crests and troughs cancel. That orbit cannot exist.

Drag the slider through non-whole numbers and watch the red line, which is what is left after the laps are added. It survives only at n = 1, 2, 3, …

The rule turns into Bohr's condition in one line. Put λ = h / mv into nλ = 2πr:

n · h / (mv) = 2πr ⇒ L = mvr = n · h / 2π

The angular momentum L of the electron can only be a whole multiple of h / 2π. Combine this with the Coulomb pull of the nucleus providing the centripetal force, and the radius and energy of each orbit follow (Unit 3).

KEY POINTS
  • Classical orbits collapse in about 10−11 s and would give a continuous spectrum. Real atoms are stable and give sharp lines.
  • An electron wave survives only if a whole number of wavelengths fits around the orbit: nλn = 2πrn.
  • With λ = h / mv this is L = mvr = nh / 2π, Bohr's quantization of angular momentum. n = 1, 2, 3, … is the principal quantum number.
  • Quantization is not an extra assumption once the electron is a wave. It is the same reason a guitar string has notes.

03

Energy levels and the hydrogen spectrum

rn = n²a0 / Z En = −13.6 Z² / n² eV

Set the Coulomb pull equal to the centripetal force, mv² / r = Ze² / (4πε0r²), and use mvr = nh / 2π to remove v. Only these radii and energies are allowed:

Radiusgrows as n²
rn = n² h² ε0 / (Zπme²) = n² a0 / Z, with the Bohr radius a0 = 0.0529 nm.
Energynegative, crowds toward 0
En = −Ze² / (8πε0 rn) = −13.6 Z² / n² eV. Negative means bound: it takes energy to pull the electron out. E = 0 is a free electron at rest far away.

An electron can only jump between levels. Jumping down, it gives the difference away as one photon; jumping up, it has to absorb exactly that difference. The photon energy fixes its wavelength:

ΔE = hc / λ ⇒ λ (nm) = 1240 / ΔE (eV)

Because only certain ΔE exist, only certain wavelengths appear. Pick a jump and watch both the orbit picture and the level diagram.

04

Matter waves and uncertainty

λ = h / p Δx · Δp ≥ h / 4π

De Broglie's idea is that any moving particle with momentum p has a wavelength λ = h / p, with Planck's constant h = 6.63 × 10−34 J·s. For everyday objects λ is absurdly small. For an electron it is about the size of an atom, so inside atoms the wave behavior decides everything.

4-1A particle made of waves: the wave packet

A single wave of one wavelength goes on forever, so it says nothing about where the particle is. Schrödinger's approach is to build the particle out of many waves. Waves with slightly different wavelengths agree in one region and cancel elsewhere, which gives a wave packet. The wave function ψ is the amplitude, and |ψ|² is the probability density of finding the particle there.

To squeeze the packet into a smaller region you have to mix in a wider range of wavelengths, that is, a wider range of momenta. Position and momentum cannot both be sharp: Δx · Δp ≥ h / 4π, the Heisenberg uncertainty principle. This is why an electron in an atom cannot follow a definite path, and why Unit 5 draws a cloud.

Spread of wavelengths mixed in, Δk ·
KEY POINTS
  • λ = h / p for every moving particle. For electrons in atoms λ is about atomic size, so wave behavior cannot be ignored.
  • ψ is a probability amplitude. |ψ|² = ψψ* is the probability density.
  • A narrow packet needs a wide spread of momenta: Δx · Δp ≥ h / 4π. Electrons in atoms have no trajectories, only probability clouds.

05

The electron cloud: orbitals

ψnlm(r, θ, φ) = Rnl(r) · Ylm(θ, φ)

Solve Schrödinger's equation for the hydrogen atom and the standing-wave condition of Unit 2 comes back, now in three dimensions. A wave in 3D has to fit in three directions, so it needs three whole numbers instead of one:

nprincipal
Fits the wave in the radial direction. Sets the energy (−13.6 / n² eV in hydrogen) and the overall size, about n² a0.
lorbital, 0 … n−1
How many times the wave changes sign going around: the shape. l = 0, 1, 2, 3 are called s, p, d, f.
mlmagnetic, −l … +l
Which way the shape points. There are 2l + 1 of them: one s, three p, five d, seven f.

The result is an orbital: not a path, but a region where |ψ|² tells you how likely the electron is to be found. Below, each dot is one place the electron could turn up. Where dots are dense, the electron is likely to be. Where the wave changes sign (blue to orange), |ψ|² drops to zero, and there is a node with no dots at all.

The curve above is the radial probability P(r) = r²Rnl²: the chance of finding the electron in a thin shell at distance r, whatever the direction. It links the cloud back to Bohr. For 1s the peak sits exactly at a0, Bohr's first radius. Bohr got the size right, but the electron is spread over a range of r rather than fixed on a circle. The number of humps is n − l, so the s orbitals of high n have small inner humps close to the nucleus. Unit 6 shows why that matters.

KEY POINTS
  • An orbital is a standing wave in 3D. Three quantum numbers fix it: n (size and energy), l (shape: s p d f), ml (orientation).
  • Dots are dense where |ψ|² is large. Where ψ changes sign there is a node with |ψ|² = 0.
  • Total nodes = n − 1: radial nodes (spheres) n − l − 1, plus angular nodes (planes or cones) l.
  • In hydrogen all orbitals with the same n have the same energy. In atoms with more electrons this is no longer true (Unit 6).
  • A fourth number, the spin ms = ±½, does not change the shape. It lets each orbital hold two electrons.

06

Filling the orbitals: electron configuration

1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p

An atom with Z electrons puts them into orbitals following three rules:

Lowest energy first
Fill the lowest available level before the next one (aufbau).
Pauli exclusion
No two electrons share all four quantum numbers, so each orbital holds at most two, with opposite spin (↑↓). A shell n holds 2n² electrons.
Hund's rule
In a set of equal-energy orbitals (the three p, the five d), electrons spread out one per orbital with parallel spins before any pairs up.

With more than one electron, orbitals of the same n no longer have the same energy. Inner electrons shield the outer ones from the full nuclear charge. An orbital that spends some time close to the nucleus feels more of that charge and drops lower. The 4s orbital has small humps deep inside (look at the curve below), while 3d stays outside. So 4s fills before 3d, even though n = 4 > 3.

KEY POINTS
  • Fill order: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p. Each orbital holds two electrons; shell n holds 2n².
  • Noble gases (He 1s², Ne … Kr ending in s²p⁶) have full outer shells: high ionization energy, no electron affinity, chemically quiet.
  • Transition metals lose 4s electrons first when they form cations: Fe [Ar]3d⁶4s², Fe2+ [Ar]3d⁶, Fe3+ [Ar]3d⁵. Last in is not first out.
  • Cr and Cu are exceptions: [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ and [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹, because a half-full or full d set is extra stable.

07

Electronegativity: who pulls harder

% ionic = 1 − exp(−¼ (XA − XB)²)

The electron configuration decides how an atom behaves in a reaction. Atoms with one or two electrons beyond a full shell give them away easily and become cations: they are electropositive (Na, K, Ca). Atoms one or two electrons short of a full shell grab electrons and become anions: they are electronegative (F, O, Cl).

Electronegativity X measures how strongly an atom pulls shared electrons toward itself, on a scale from about 0.7 (Fr, Cs) to 4.0 (F). It rises to the right across a period and up a group. When two atoms bond, the difference ΔX decides the bond type: large ΔX means the electron is transferred (ionic), small ΔX means it is shared (covalent). Pauling's formula turns ΔX into a percentage of ionic character.

KEY POINTS
  • Electronegativity rises toward the upper right of the periodic table (F = 4.0) and falls toward the lower left (Cs, Fr ≈ 0.7). Noble gases are left out.
  • Most real bonds are mixed. GaAs (ΔX = 0.4) is 4% ionic, ZnSe (ΔX = 0.8) is 15% ionic, NaCl (ΔX = 2.1) is about 67% ionic.
  • Two different elements always give some ionic character. Only bonds between identical atoms (Si–Si, H–H) are purely covalent.

08

Ionic bonds: one curve explains many properties

Enet = Z1Z2e² / (4πε0a) + b / an

Na (3s¹) gives its electron to Cl (3s²3p⁵). Both ions now have noble-gas configurations, Na+ like Ne and Cl− like Ar, and they attract each other by Coulomb's law. They cannot merge, because when their filled electron shells start to overlap a strong repulsion sets in. Two terms compete as a function of the distance a between the ion centers:

Attractionlong range
Fatt = −Z1Z2e² / (4πε0a²), energy Z1Z2e² / (4πε0a), which is negative because Z1Z2 < 0.
Repulsionshort range
Frep = −nb / an+1, energy b / an, with n = 7 to 10. It only matters when a is small.

The forces balance where Fnet = 0, which is the same place Enet is lowest: the equilibrium spacing a0. Energy is the integral of force, so a zero of the force curve is a minimum of the energy curve. The shape of this one curve predicts how the material behaves.

8-1How many anions fit around a cation: radius ratio

An ionic bond pulls equally in every direction, so an ionic solid packs as many anions around each cation as it can, while keeping the whole crystal neutral. The limit is geometry. The anions must touch the cation. If the cation is too small for a given arrangement, the anions bump into each other before they reach it, the cation rattles in its cage, and the structure is unstable. The smallest cation that still touches all its neighbors gives the critical radius ratio for each coordination number (CN).

  • Lattice energy and melting point are high in ionic solids. They rise with ionic charge (MgO ≫ NaCl) and fall as the ions get larger.

09

Covalent bonds: sharing electron clouds

sp³ = ½ (s ± px ± py ± pz) 109.5°

When two atoms of similar electronegativity meet, neither can take the other's electron. Instead their outer orbitals overlap and a pair of electrons is shared, so that each atom counts a full shell. H2 shares one pair. F2 (2s²2p⁵) shares one p electron each, O2 two, N2 three. Because the bond lives where two orbitals overlap, a covalent bond points in a definite direction.

Carbon is the key case. Its ground state 1s²2s²2p² has only two half-filled orbitals, which suggests two bonds. Yet carbon forms four, all alike, at 109.5° to each other. The reason is hybridization: one 2s electron is promoted to 2p, and the 2s and three 2p waves mix into four equal sp³ orbitals. Adding waves means some regions add (same sign) and others cancel (opposite sign). Build them step by step below.

KEY POINTS
  • Covalent bonds form between atoms of similar electronegativity by sharing electron pairs. They are directional, because the bond is where two orbitals overlap.
  • More shared pairs → stronger and shorter bonds.
  • sp³ hybrids point to the corners of a tetrahedron, 109.5° apart. In diamond every carbon bonds to four others this way: very hard, bond energy 711 kJ/mol, melting point 3550 °C.
  • Strong bonds inside a molecule do not make a strong solid. CH4 has four strong C–H bonds, but the molecules hold each other only by weak secondary bonds, so methane melts at −183 °C.

10

Metallic bonds and mixed bonds

ion cores in a sea of shared electrons

Metal atoms hold their outer electrons loosely and have few of them. Packed closely in a crystal, each atom's valence electrons are attracted to the neighboring nuclei as much as to their own, and they spread through the whole crystal as an electron cloud shared by all the positive ion cores. Two things follow:

Conduction
The shared electrons move freely, so a small voltage drives a current. The same free electrons carry heat.
Ductility
The bond is non-directional: an ion core is happy wherever it sits in the electron sea. Planes of atoms can slide past each other without breaking the bond.

Compare that with an ionic crystal. Slide one layer by one ion-to-ion spacing and every ion faces an ion of the same charge. The layers push apart and the crystal cleaves. That is why metals bend and ceramics shatter.

KEY POINTS
  • Metallic bond: positive ion cores held together by a shared cloud of free valence electrons. Non-directional.
  • Free electrons → electrical and thermal conduction. Non-directional bond → layers slide without breaking → ductility.
  • In an ionic crystal a slide of one ion spacing brings like charges face to face, so ceramics are brittle.

11

Secondary bonds: dipoles

μ = qd fluctuating · permanent · hydrogen bond

Atoms with full shells and molecules whose bonds are already satisfied still attract each other a little. The attraction comes from electric dipoles: wherever the centers of positive and negative charge separate by a distance d, there is a dipole moment μ = qd, and dipoles pull on each other. Secondary bonds are much weaker than primary bonds, but they decide whether a molecular substance is a gas, a liquid or a solid at room temperature.

Fluctuating dipoles
The electron cloud of even a noble-gas atom is not always centered. For an instant it shifts, the atom is a dipole, and it pushes the cloud of its neighbor into a matching shift. Bigger atoms have looser clouds, larger dipoles, and higher melting and boiling points.
Permanent dipoles
A molecule whose bonds are polar and not arranged symmetrically carries a fixed dipole. CH4 is symmetric and has none; CH3Cl has one.
Hydrogen bonds
The strongest secondary bond. H bonded to O, N or F is stripped nearly bare and attracts lone pairs on a neighboring molecule. Water's bent shape (about 105°) gives it a large dipole.
Kind of secondary bond
KEY POINTS
  • Strength, roughly: ionic, covalent, metallic (100 – 1000 kJ/mol) ≫ hydrogen bond (about 20 – 40) > permanent dipole > fluctuating dipole (a few).
  • Noble gases are held only by fluctuating dipoles, and their boiling points rise with size: He 4 K, Ne 27 K, Ar 87 K, Kr 120 K, Xe 165 K, Rn 211 K.
  • Symmetric molecules cancel their bond dipoles (CH4). Asymmetric ones keep a net dipole (CH3Cl, H2O).
