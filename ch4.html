01

From liquid to grains

nucleation → growth → grain boundaries

Molten metal does not freeze all at once. It freezes in two steps.

Nucleation
Here and there in the liquid, a small group of atoms lines up into a crystal. A group that is large enough to survive is a nucleus.
Crystal growth
Liquid atoms attach to each nucleus, so each one grows until it runs into its neighbors. In metals the growing crystal usually branches like a tree, and such a crystal is called a dendrite.

Every nucleus starts with its own orientation. When two growing crystals meet, their lattices do not line up, and the mismatched seam between them is a grain boundary. Each crystal is now called a grain, and the solid is a polycrystal.

The number of nuclei sets the grain size. More nuclei means more grains, and each grain has less room, so the grains are smaller. Foundries use this. A little Ti, B or Zr added to molten aluminum gives the liquid many places to nucleate, and the casting comes out fine-grained. These additions are called grain refiners.

Where the nuclei form matters too. The mold wall is cold, so many nuclei form there and give small equiaxed grains, about the same size in every direction. Further in, the liquid cools slowly and heat flows out through the wall, so the grains that already exist grow inward along that direction and become long columnar grains.

1-1Why the liquid has to be colder than Tm: the driving force

Pure water in a clean bottle can sit in a freezer below 0 °C and stay liquid. Tap the bottle and it turns to ice within seconds. Tiny droplets of pure water stay liquid down to about −40 °C. A liquid below its melting point is undercooled, and how far below is the undercooling ΔT = Tm − T. To see why undercooling matters, compare the free energy of the two phases.

General chemistry gave the rule: at fixed temperature and pressure, the phase with the lower Gibbs free energy G = H − TS is the stable one. Plot G against T for the solid and for the liquid. Near Tm both are close to straight lines.

Both lines slope down
A higher T makes the −TS term more negative. The slope of each line is −S.
The liquid line is steeper
Liquid atoms are disordered, so the liquid has more entropy than the solid.
They cross at Tm
There the two phases have the same G, so ΔG = 0 and they can coexist. Above Tm the liquid line is lower and the liquid is stable. Below Tm the solid line is lower.

At Tm, ΔG = ΔHf − TmΔS = 0, so ΔS = ΔHf / Tm. This is the same step general chemistry uses for a boiling point, Tb = ΔHvap / ΔSvap. Below Tm, the gap between the two lines is the driving force for freezing. Two straight lines that cross move apart in proportion to the distance from the crossing: the gap is the difference in slope times ΔT, that is ΔS · ΔT. Put in ΔS and you get the formula on the slides. Drag the temperature and watch the gap.

KEY POINTS
  • The phase with the lower G is stable. The G lines of solid and liquid cross at Tm. The liquid line is steeper because the liquid has more entropy.
  • At Tm: ΔS = ΔHf / Tm. Below Tm: |ΔGv| ≈ ΔS · ΔT = ΔHf ΔT / Tm. The driving force grows in proportion to the undercooling.
  • Watch the sign. The slides write ΔGv ≈ ΔHf ΔT / Tm, the size of the gap, with the latent heat ΔHf taken as positive. For liquid → solid, ΔGv = GS − GL is negative, so Unit 2 writes ΔGv ≈ −ΔHf ΔT / Tm.
  • Watch the units. ΔHf here is per unit volume (J/cm³), so ΔGv comes out in J/cm³. The “entropy of fusion ≈ R” on the slides counts the same entropy per mole instead.
  • The straight lines assume ΔH and ΔS do not change with temperature, which holds while ΔT is small compared with Tm. A driving force alone still does not make the liquid freeze. Unit 2 shows the price a new crystal has to pay first.

02

Why a nucleus has to be big enough

ΔGT = volume term + surface term

Below the melting point the solid has the lower energy, so one might expect the liquid to freeze at once. It does not, because a small crystal has to pay for its surface.

Volume termnegative
Every bit of liquid that turns solid releases energy. For a sphere of radius r this is (4/3)πr³ · ΔGv, and ΔGv is negative.
Surface termpositive
The new boundary between solid and liquid costs energy γ per unit area. For the same sphere this is 4πr² · γ.

A tiny cluster is almost all surface, so the cost wins and the cluster dissolves again. Such a cluster is an embryo. Volume grows as r³ and surface only as r², so beyond a certain size the gain wins and the cluster keeps growing. That size is the critical radius r*, and a cluster larger than r* is a nucleus.

Cooling further below the melting point makes ΔGv more negative, which makes r* smaller. The amount of cooling below the melting point is the undercooling ΔT. Move the two sliders and watch the same thing in the model and on the curve.

03

Nucleating on a surface

ΔG*het = ΔG*hom · f(θ)

The last unit assumed the nucleus forms alone in the middle of the liquid. This is homogeneous nucleation, and for copper it needs an undercooling of more than 200 K. Real castings freeze after only a few degrees of undercooling, because the nucleus forms on something that is already there: the mold wall, an insoluble impurity, a grain refiner. This is heterogeneous nucleation, and the surface it uses is called a nucleating agent.

On a surface the nucleus is a cap cut from a sphere. The cap has the same radius of curvature r* as the full sphere, yet it holds far fewer atoms, so far fewer atoms have to come together by chance.

How much of the sphere is left depends on the contact angle θ between the solid and the surface. The angle comes from a balance of three surface energies (Young's equation): γna–L = γna–S + γSL cos θ. The better the solid wets the nucleating agent, the smaller θ is and the flatter the cap. Surface energy and surface tension are the same quantity here: the energy per unit area equals the force per unit length of edge.

KEY POINTS
  • Shape factor: f(θ) = (2 − 3 cos θ + cos³ θ) / 4. It runs from 0 (θ = 0°) to 1 (θ = 180°).
  • Homogeneous and heterogeneous nucleation have the same r*. The heterogeneous nucleus has fewer atoms, N*het = N*hom · f(θ), and a lower barrier, ΔG*het = ΔG*hom · f(θ).
  • At θ = 180° the solid does not wet the surface at all, and the surface gives no help.

04

Solid solutions and the gaps between atoms

substitutional · interstitial · high-entropy

Most metals in use are alloys, mixtures of two or more elements. Cartridge brass is 70% Cu and 30% Zn. Inconel, a nickel-based superalloy, holds about 10 elements. The simplest kind of alloy is a solid solution: the added atoms (the solute) are spread through the crystal of the main element (the solvent), and the whole thing is still one phase with one crystal structure.

A solute atom can sit in two kinds of place.

Substitutional
The solute takes the place of a solvent atom on a lattice site. The lattice is slightly distorted, but the structure does not change.
Interstitial
A small atom such as C, N or H sits in a gap between the solvent atoms. The gaps are called interstitial sites.

How much solute a substitutional solution can hold is its solid solubility. The Hume-Rothery rules say when it is high:

  • The atomic radii differ by no more than 15%.
  • The two elements have similar crystal structures.
  • They have similar electronegativity.
  • They have the same valence. This is needed for complete solubility.

4-1Mixing many elements: configurational entropy

Mixing is decided by ΔGmix = ΔHmix − T ΔSmix. The more ways there are to arrange the atoms, the larger the entropy ΔSmix, and the lower the free energy of the mixed state. For n elements in equal amounts, the entropy from arrangement alone is ΔSconf = R ln n, with R = 8.31 J/mol·K.

A high-entropy alloy (HEA) has at least 5 main elements, each between 5 and 35 atomic percent. The entropy is then large enough to favor one mixed solid solution over a set of separate compounds.

When the amounts are not equal, the formula is ΔSconf = −R Σ xi ln xi, where xi is the atomic fraction of each element. By this measure most traditional alloys are low-entropy. 7-3 brass is 0.61R and 304 stainless steel is 0.96R. Nickel-base superalloys such as Inconel 718 reach 1.31R, which counts as medium.

05

Point defects

nv / N = C · exp(−Ev / kT)

No real crystal is perfect. Defects are sorted by how many dimensions they extend in:

  • 0-D, point defects: vacancies, interstitials. This unit.
  • 1-D, line defects: dislocations. Unit 6.
  • 2-D, planar defects: grain boundaries, stacking faults, twin boundaries, surfaces. Unit 7.
  • 3-D, volume defects: cracks, voids, precipitates.

A point defect is a mistake at one or two atomic sites. The atoms around it shift a little, so each point defect carries a small region of strain.

Vacancy
A lattice site with no atom on it. Vacancies form during solidification and during plastic deformation, rapid cooling or particle bombardment. Vacancies that gather together become microvoids.
Self-interstitial
An atom of the crystal itself squeezed into a gap. It distorts the lattice strongly, so it mostly appears after high-energy irradiation or ion bombardment.
Impurity atom
A foreign atom, either on a lattice site or in a gap. These are the solute atoms of Unit 4.
Schottky defect
In an ionic crystal, one cation and one anion missing together. Removing them as a pair keeps the crystal electrically neutral.
Frenkel defect
In an ionic crystal, a cation that has left its site and moved into a gap nearby. It leaves a vacancy behind.
In a metal

5-1How many vacancies there are

Vacancies are the one defect a crystal cannot get rid of. Making a vacancy costs an energy Ev of about 1 eV, but a few vacancies raise the entropy, so at any temperature above absolute zero some are always present. Their fraction rises steeply with temperature: nv / N = C · exp(−Ev / kT), where nv is the number of vacancies, N is the number of atom sites and k = 8.62 × 10⁻⁵ eV/K.

06

Dislocations: line defects

edge: b ⟂ line screw: b ∥ line

To bend a metal, one part of the crystal has to slide over another. If a whole plane of atoms had to slide at once, every bond across that plane would break at the same moment, and metals would be far stronger than they are. Real crystals slide one row at a time. The boundary between the part that has already slipped and the part that has not is a line, and that line is a dislocation. Dislocations form during solidification, during plastic deformation, and when vacancies collapse together.

Edge dislocation
An extra half-plane of atoms ends inside the crystal. The dislocation is the line along the bottom edge of that half-plane.
Screw dislocation
The crystal is cut part of the way through and one side is pushed along the cut. The atomic planes turn into one continuous spiral ramp around the line.
Mixed dislocation
Part edge and part screw. A curved dislocation line is screw where it runs along the slip direction, edge where it runs across it, and mixed in between.

The Burgers vector b measures the slip. Walk a closed loop of atom-to-atom steps in a perfect crystal and you return to the start. Walk the same loop around a dislocation and you end up one step away. The step needed to close the loop is b. For an edge dislocation b is perpendicular to the line. For a screw dislocation it is parallel to the line.

KEY POINTS
  • Edge: b ⟂ dislocation line. Screw: b ∥ dislocation line. Mixed: in between.
  • Around an edge dislocation the side with the extra half-plane is squeezed and the other side is stretched. Around a screw dislocation there is only shear. This strain field is how dislocations feel each other and feel solute atoms.
  • When a dislocation has crossed the whole slip plane, one half of the crystal has moved by exactly b and the lattice inside is perfect again.

6-1Schmid's law: how much of the pull acts on the slip plane

A dislocation moves when the shear stress on its slip plane, along its slip direction, is large enough. The smallest shear stress that makes a single crystal slip is the critical resolved shear stress (CRSS). It depends on the crystal structure, the bonding, the temperature and the purity.

Pull on a crystal with a tensile stress σ. Only part of it acts as shear on a given slip system. Two angles decide how much: φ between the pull and the normal of the slip plane, and λ between the pull and the slip direction.

07

Planar defects

grain boundary · stacking fault · twin

A planar defect is a whole surface across which the crystal does not continue perfectly.

Free surface
Surface atoms have neighbors on one side only, so they sit at a higher energy and react easily. Nanomaterials are mostly surface, which is why they are so reactive.
Grain boundary
The seam between two grains of different orientation. In a tilt boundary the rotation axis lies in the boundary. In a twist boundary it is perpendicular to the boundary.
Stacking fault
One close-packed layer is missing or one extra layer is inserted, so the ABCABC order is broken at one place.
Twin boundary
The stacking order reverses, and the crystal on one side is the mirror image of the other. Every atom on the boundary still fits both sides, so it is a coherent boundary with low energy.

A grain boundary with a small tilt angle is simply a row of edge dislocations. Each dislocation adds one half-plane and turns the lattice a little more. The larger the angle, the closer the dislocations have to be.

08

Grain size and strength

N = 2n−1 σy = σ0 + k / √d

To see grains under an optical microscope, the sample is ground and polished flat and then etched with a chemical. The etchant attacks grain boundaries faster than the grains and leaves a small groove at each one. The groove scatters light away from the lens, so the boundary shows as a dark line. Grains of different orientation are also etched at different rates, so some grains look darker than others.

Two numbers describe the grain size.

ASTM grain size number n
Count the grains in one square inch of a micrograph taken at 100×. If the count is N, then N = 2n−1. A larger n means finer grains.
Average grain diameter d
Draw a line of known length on the micrograph and count the grains it crosses. With nL grains per unit length of line and a magnification M, d = C / (nL M), with C = 1.5.

A grain boundary stops dislocations, because the slip plane does not continue into the next grain. Smaller grains mean more boundary area per volume and a stronger metal. The Hall–Petch equation fits this: σy = σ0 + k / √d. Many small grains pointing in random directions also make the properties the same in every direction.

Microscopes: how the defects are seen

OM SEM TEM STM AFM

A microscope cannot show details much smaller than the wavelength of what it uses to look. Visible light has a wavelength of about 500 nm, and atoms are about 0.25 nm apart, so an optical microscope shows grains but never atoms. Two ways around this limit are used: electrons, which have a far shorter wavelength, and a sharp tip that feels the surface directly.

9-1Why electrons see more than light

An electron is also a wave. Its wavelength is λ = h / p = h / √(2mE), so the higher the accelerating voltage, the shorter the wavelength. In an SEM the beam is scanned over a small area and the signal is drawn on a much larger screen. The magnification is simply the width of the screen divided by the width of the scanned area. A 10 cm screen showing a 10 µm scan is 10,000×.

A TEM does not reach its wavelength, because magnetic lenses are far from perfect. The resolution is about Δx = 0.6 Cs1/4 λ3/4, where Cs measures the spherical aberration of the lens. At 100 keV this gives about 2 Å, which is still enough to see rows of atoms.
