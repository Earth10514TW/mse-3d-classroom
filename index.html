01

Lattice and unit cell

a, b, c / α, β, γ

When the atoms in a solid are lined up in order and the same arrangement repeats over and over, the solid is called a crystal. Iron, copper, silicon and table salt are crystals. In glass the atoms have no regular order, so glass is not a crystal.

Describing this repetition takes two ideas.

Lattice
Ignore what the atoms look like for now and use points to mark where each repeating unit sits. These points are called lattice points.
Basisor motif
The group of atoms placed on every lattice point. It may be 1 atom or several.

Put the two together and you have crystal structure = lattice + basis. The unit cell is the smallest repeating block that can stand for the whole lattice. Once you know its three edge lengths a, b, c and its three angles α, β, γ, you can copy and paste it to build the whole crystal. These 6 numbers are called the lattice parameters.

Picture a tiled wall. The crossings of the grout lines are the lattice. The pattern on each tile is the basis. One tile is the unit cell. However fancy the pattern is, once you know one tile you can cover the whole wall.

02

Seven crystal systems and 14 Bravais lattices

7 crystal systems → 14 lattices

Unit cells are sorted by two things: whether the edges a, b, c are equal, and whether the angles α, β, γ are 90°. Sorting this way gives only 7 classes, called the seven crystal systems.

Next comes where the lattice points can go. Besides the 8 corners, a cell can hold a few more points, as long as every point still has exactly the same surroundings afterwards. Only four placements pass this test:

  • Simple P: only the 8 corners.
  • Body-centered I: one extra point at the very center of the cell.
  • Face-centered F: one extra point at the center of each of the 6 faces.
  • Base-centered C: one extra point at the center of the top and bottom faces only.

Pair every crystal system with every placement and take out the duplicates. The French scientist Auguste Bravais (1811–1863) showed that only 14 are left, so they are called the Bravais lattices. The buttons on the panel switch through them one at a time.

03

The three main crystal structures of metals

APF 0.52 / 0.68 / 0.74

About 90% of metals have one of three structures: BCC, FCC or HCP. The metallic bond has no fixed direction, so you can think of the atoms as hard spheres that touch each other and pack together as tightly as they can. To compare these structures we look at the numbers N, CN and APF:

Atoms per unit cell N
A corner atom is shared by 8 cells, so it counts as 1/8. A face atom is shared by 2 cells and counts as 1/2. An atom fully inside the cell counts as 1.
Coordination number CN
The number of nearest neighbors touching one atom.
Atomic packing factor APF
The fraction of the cell volume taken up by atoms (hard spheres). The larger the APF, the tighter the packing.
APF = (atoms in the cell × sphere volume) ÷ cell volume = N · (4/3)πR³ ÷ Vcell

04

Two ways to stack close-packed layers

…ABAB… / …ABCABC…

Pack equal spheres as tightly as possible on a flat surface and each sphere ends up with exactly 6 around it. Call this layer A. The gaps between the spheres are hollows, and the hollows fall into two sets. We call them the B positions and the C positions.

The second layer has to drop into one set of hollows. Call it layer B. The two structures part ways at the third layer:

  • Third layer directly above the first → ABAB…, which is HCP.
  • Third layer in the third kind of position, C → ABCABC…, which is FCC.

The two stackings are equally tight, so both have an APF of 0.74 and a coordination number of 12. In the cubic cell of FCC, these close-packed planes are the (111) planes, and they stack along the body diagonal [111].

05

Common semiconductor lattices

Si GaAs NaCl GaN

In semiconductors such as silicon (Si), germanium (Ge), gallium arsenide (GaAs) and gallium nitride (GaN), the atoms are joined by covalent bonds. A covalent bond has a fixed direction. Each atom bonds to only 4 neighbors, and those 4 neighbors sit at the corners of a regular tetrahedron. So these crystals are not packed as tightly as metals, and the APF of the diamond structure is only about 0.34.

All four structures can be seen as two lattices pushed into each other. In the models, blue marks the first set of sites and orange marks the second.

Miller indices: positions, directions and planes

[uvw] (hkl) <uvw> {hkl}

To say which direction to pull along or which plane to cut along, everyone needs the same notation. That notation is the Miller indices. Directions and planes in this unit use a cubic cell, with the edge length a taken as 1.

Atom positions
Written as fractional coordinates (x, y, z). The body center, for example, is (½, ½, ½). The model lists them for every structure in Units 3 and 5. HCP and wurtzite use the hexagonal axes a1, a2, c.
Direction [uvw]
Subtract the start coordinates from the end coordinates, reduce to the smallest integers, and use square brackets. A minus sign is written above the number, as in [110], read “one, bar one, zero”. A whole family of directions that are equivalent by symmetry is written <uvw>. For example, <100> covers 6 directions.
Plane (hkl)
① Find the intercepts of the plane on the x, y and z axes. ② Take their reciprocals. ③ Clear the fractions to get integers, and use round brackets. When the plane is parallel to an axis, the intercept is ∞ and its reciprocal is 0. When the plane passes through the origin, move the origin to a neighboring corner first. A family of equivalent planes is written {hkl}.

Four-index notation for hexagonal crystals

u + v + t = 0 h + k + i = 0

The base of a hexagonal cell has three axes a1, a2, a3 at 120° to each other, plus the vertical c axis. With only three indices, six directions that are equivalent by symmetry come out as numbers that look unrelated. With the four Miller–Bravais indices [uvtw] and (hkil), equivalent directions and planes differ only by numbers changing places and changing sign.

Because a3 = −(a1 + a2), the four indices are not independent. A direction must satisfy u + v + t = 0, and a plane must satisfy h + k + i = 0.

09

Seeing how atoms are arranged with X‑ray diffraction

2d sinθ = nλ

Atoms are too small to see with an ordinary microscope. Visible light has a wavelength of 400–700 nm, more than a thousand times the spacing between atoms (about 0.2–0.3 nm), so it cannot tell one layer of atoms from the next. Shine light whose wavelength is about the same as the spacing between atoms on a crystal instead. The light scattered by layer after layer of atoms interferes, and it turns bright only at certain angles. Measure those angles and you can work back to how the atoms are arranged. X‑rays have wavelengths of about 0.05–0.25 nm, the same scale as the spacing between crystal planes.

This unit goes in order: how two waves add (9‑1), where X‑rays come from (9‑2), which angles turn bright (9‑3), how an instrument measures them (9‑4), why some peaks go missing (9‑5), and how to read a pattern back into a crystal structure (9‑6 to 9‑8).

9-1When two waves meet: reinforce or cancel

X‑rays are waves, like ripples on water. The distance from one crest to the next is the wavelength λ. Everything in this unit rests on one fact: when two waves meet, their heights add.

In phase
Crest meets crest. The two waves reinforce each other and the result is twice as tall. This is called constructive interference.
Out of phase
Crest meets trough. The two waves cancel and nothing is left. This is called destructive interference.

Which one you get depends on how far one wave lags behind the other. A lag of 0, 1, 2 … whole wavelengths puts the two back in step. A lag of ½, 1½ … wavelengths puts them exactly opposite. Drag the slider and watch the sum.

9-2Where X‑rays come from

Inside the vacuum of an X‑ray tube, a heated tungsten filament (the cathode) gives off electrons, and a metal cup around it focuses them into a beam. A high voltage of tens of thousands of volts (35 kV in the lecture's figure) accelerates them into a metal target (the anode, usually Cu, Mo or W). About 99% of their energy turns into heat, so the target is cooled with water. Some tubes also spin the anode, so the beam keeps landing on a fresh, cool spot. The remaining 1% or so leaves as X‑rays. Two kinds come out together.

Continuous X‑rays
An electron that passes close to a nucleus in the target is pulled off course and slowed down. The energy it loses leaves as an X‑ray photon. Each electron is slowed by a different amount: a little, a lot, or stopped completely. So the wavelengths spread over a continuous range, like a broad hump.
Characteristic X‑rays
An electron can also knock an inner electron (K shell) out of a target atom. An outer electron drops down to fill the hole and gives off the energy difference as one X‑ray photon. A drop from the L shell gives Kα. A drop from the M shell gives Kβ. The shell energies are fixed by the element, so these wavelengths identify the target: Cu Kα is 0.154 nm and Mo Kα is 0.071 nm. Looked at closely, Kα is two lines very close together, Kα1 and Kα2, because the L shell has sublevels. They are usually treated as one line.

The energy of a photon and its wavelength are tied by one handy number: E (eV) = 1240 ÷ λ (nm). An electron accelerated through V volts carries V electron-volts. When all of that goes into one photon, you get the shortest wavelength in the spectrum: λmin(nm) = 1240 ÷ accelerating voltage (V). At 35 kV that is 1240 ÷ 35000 = 0.035 nm. Older figures use the ångström (1 Å = 0.1 nm), and there the same rule reads E (eV) = 12400 ÷ λ (Å).

Diffraction needs a single wavelength, so experiments use the strong Kα line. A thin metal filter (nickel foil for a copper tube) removes most of the Kβ.

9-3Bragg's law: the angles that turn bright

Think of a crystal as a stack of parallel planes of atoms, a distance d apart. An X‑ray beam comes in at an angle θ to the planes. Note that θ is measured from the plane itself, not from the line perpendicular to it. Every plane scatters a little of the beam.

Compare the ray scattered by the first layer with the ray scattered by the second. The lower ray has to go down to the second layer and come back up, so it travels farther. The extra distance is the two red segments in the figure, FG and GH. Each one is a side of a right triangle whose longest side is OG = d, so each has length d sinθ. Together, FG + GH = 2d sinθ.

From 9‑1, the two rays reinforce only when the lag is a whole number of wavelengths:

2d sinθ = nλ  (n = 1, 2, 3 …)

This is Bragg's law. The whole number n is the order of diffraction. n = 1 means the lower ray is exactly one wavelength behind, n = 2 means two. Drag the sliders to change θ, d and λ, and watch for when the crests of the two waves line up.

FOUR THINGS TO NOTICE
A worked case
The (111) planes of copper are d = 0.2087 nm apart. With Cu Kα (λ = 0.154 nm) and n = 1, sinθ = λ ÷ 2d = 0.154 ÷ 0.4174 = 0.369, so θ = 21.7°.
The wavelength must be short enough
sinθ can never be more than 1, so diffraction needs λ ≤ 2d. Visible light is far too long, which is why a crystal does not diffract it.
Why real peaks are sharp
With only two layers, the brightness fades slowly as you move off the Bragg angle. A real crystal has thousands of layers. Slightly off the angle, every layer finds a deeper layer that is exactly half a wavelength behind it, and the two cancel. So the peak is very narrow.
We usually set n = 1
Second-order diffraction (n = 2) from (100) planes of spacing d falls at the same angle as first-order diffraction from planes of spacing d/2, and those are the (200) planes. So the order is folded into the indices and the law is written λ = 2 dhkl sinθ, with dhkl from unit 6.

9-4How a diffraction pattern is measured

The instrument is called a diffractometer. The X‑ray beam hits the sample at an angle θ to its surface. The detector waits on the other side, also at θ to the surface. Seen from the incoming beam, the detector has turned through 2θ, and 2θ is the angle the instrument records. That is why the horizontal axis of every diffraction pattern is 2θ and not θ.

During a scan the sample turns by θ while the detector turns by 2θ, so the two angles stay matched. At most angles the scattered waves cancel and the detector counts almost nothing. When some set of planes satisfies Bragg's law, the count jumps. Plot the count against 2θ and you have the diffraction pattern. Press Scan to see one being drawn.

The middle window zooms into one grain whose planes lie parallel to the sample surface. Only grains like this send X‑rays into the detector, because only for them are the angle in and the angle out both θ. The window shows the planes of the nearest peak, drawn as in 9‑3. Drag 2θ and watch the waves: on a peak every layer adds in phase, and just off it the layers drift out of step and cancel.

The sample in the scan above is a powder. It holds countless tiny grains pointing every which way, so for every set of planes some grains sit at exactly the Bragg angle. Their diffracted rays form a cone around the beam, and on a flat film the cones show up as rings. A single crystal has only one orientation. It gives separate bright spots, and it has to be turned on a goniometer (a holder that rotates the crystal about several axes) to bring each set of planes to its Bragg angle. A single crystal gives a full three-dimensional set of spots. A powder squeezes the same information onto one axis, 2θ. That is quicker to measure, but peaks that fall close together can overlap.

9-5The structure factor: why some peaks are missing

An X‑ray is an electromagnetic wave. Its oscillating electric field shakes the electrons of every atom it passes, and the shaken electrons send out X‑rays of the same wavelength in all directions. That is what “an atom scatters X‑rays” means. The wave leaving the crystal is the sum of the waves from all the atoms. Every unit cell is the same, so it is enough to add up the atoms of one cell.

Bragg's law counts only one layer of atoms per spacing d. A unit cell often has more atoms sitting between those layers, and they scatter X‑rays too. Their waves can make the peak stronger, or wipe it out.

Take the BCC (100) peak. At the Bragg angle, the waves from two neighboring layers of corner atoms differ by exactly one wavelength, so they are in step. The body-center atoms form one more layer, exactly halfway between. Their wave is half a wavelength behind, so its crests meet the troughs of the corner wave. The two cancel and the peak never shows up. This is called extinction (a systematic absence).

Every atom follows the same rule. An atom at (x, y, z) sends out a wave that lags by hx + ky + lz wavelengths. A whole number means in step. A half means exactly opposite.

The plot shows this twice. The first panel draws the waves themselves. The second turns each wave into an arrow: its length is the scattering strength f of the atom, and it is turned by 360° × (hx + ky + lz). Join the arrows head to tail. The red arrow from the start to the end is the total, called the structure factor F. When the arrows end where they started, F = 0 and the peak is extinct.

Textbooks write the same sum in one line. The term eiφ simply means “an arrow turned by the angle φ”.

Fhkl = Σ fn · e2πi(hxn + kyn + lzn)  diffracted intensity ∝ |Fhkl|²

Choose BCC and try (100), then (200). For (200) the body-center wave lags by one whole wavelength, and the peak comes back.

WHAT IS f?

f is the atomic scattering factor: how strongly one atom scatters X‑rays. The electrons do the scattering, so an atom with more electrons has a larger f. Gold scatters far more strongly than aluminum.

F = f · e2πi(0) + f · e2πi(h/2 + k/2 + l/2) = f [ 1 + eπi(h + k + l) ]

h + k + l odd  → F = f (1 − 1) = 0, no peak

FCC (gold, for example) has 4 atoms per cell, at (0, 0, 0), (½, ½, 0), (½, 0, ½) and (0, ½, ½):

F = f [ 1 + eπi(h + k) + eπi(h + l) + eπi(k + l) ]
h, k, l all odd or all even → every pair adds to an even number, F = f (1 + 1 + 1 + 1) = 4f
mixed, such as (100) or (110) → two of the pairs are odd, F = f (1 + 1 − 1 − 1) = 0

The diamond structure (silicon) is an FCC lattice plus a second FCC lattice shifted by (¼, ¼, ¼). Every FCC arrow gets a shifted partner, so the FCC result is multiplied by one more bracket:

F = FFCC · [ 1 + eπi(h + k + l)/2 ]
all odd → the partner is turned a quarter turn, the bracket has length √2, intensity ∝ 32f²
all even, h + k + l = 4n → the bracket is 1 + 1 = 2, F = 8f, intensity ∝ 64f²
all even, h + k + l = 4n + 2 → the bracket is 1 − 1 = 0, no peak: (200), (222), (420)

9-6XRD pattern simulator

For a cubic crystal, two formulas are already in hand: Bragg's law with n = 1, and the interplanar spacing from unit 6.

λ = 2 d sinθ  d = a ÷ √(h² + k² + l²)

Put the second into the first, then square both sides:

sin²θ = (λ² / 4a²) · (h² + k² + l²)

The lecture writes the same relation the other way round: h² + k² + l² = (4a² / λ²) · sin²θ. λ and a are the same for every peak in one pattern, so sin²θ is proportional to the whole number h² + k² + l². Planes with small indices have a large spacing d and show up at small angles. Divide the sin²θ of each peak by the sin²θ of the first peak and look at the sequence of ratios. It tells you whether the crystal is SC, BCC or FCC.

A quick test needs only the first two peaks. Their sin²θ ratio is 2 ÷ 4 = 0.5 for BCC and 3 ÷ 4 = 0.75 for FCC.

Peak positions are calculated exactly. Peak heights are only schematic. They include just the multiplicity, the Lorentz–polarization factor and a simplified fall-off in atomic scattering, enough to show the general trend.

9-7Worked example: naming an unknown metal

A powder of an unknown cubic metal, either BCC or FCC, is measured with λ = 0.154 nm. The pattern has peaks at 2θ = 40.0°, 58.0°, 73.0°, 86.8°, 100.4° and 114.7°. Which structure is it, and what is the lattice constant a?

  1. Halve each 2θ to get θ, then calculate sin²θ.
  2. Take the ratio of the first two: 0.1170 ÷ 0.2350 = 0.498. That is 0.5, so the metal is BCC. FCC would give 0.75.
  3. Give the peaks the BCC values of h² + k² + l² in order: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12. Each value names its planes, for example 2 = 1² + 1² + 0², the {110} planes.
  4. Solve for a from each peak: a = λ √(h² + k² + l²) ÷ (2 sinθ). The first peak gives 0.154 × √2 ÷ (2 × 0.3420) = 0.318 nm.
  5. Look it up. A BCC metal with a ≈ 0.317 nm is tungsten (a = 0.3165 nm).

9-8From pattern to crystal structure

  1. Prepare the sampleGrind the material into a fine powder, or use a thin film or a bulk piece.
  2. Measure the diffraction patternScan 2θ and record the intensity at each angle.
  3. Search and matchCompare the peak positions with standard patterns of known substances (such as JCPDS/ICDD cards) and pick a candidate.
  4. Determine the unit cellCheck that the candidate's cell explains every peak. If it does not, go back to step 3 and try another candidate.
  5. Identify the crystal structureWith the cell settled, the extinction rules and peak intensities give the lattice type and where the atoms are.

A peak carries three pieces of information. Each one answers a different question about the sample.

KEY POINTS
  • Waves that lag by a whole number of wavelengths reinforce. Waves that lag by half a wavelength cancel. Bragg's law and the structure factor are both this one idea.
  • The position of a peak is set by the size and shape of the unit cell (Bragg's law). Whether a peak appears and how strong it is is set by where the atoms sit in the cell (the structure factor).
  • For cubic crystals sin²θ is proportional to h² + k² + l². The first two peaks give a ratio of 0.5 for BCC and 0.75 for FCC.
  • Silicon has the diamond structure. Its lattice is FCC, yet the (200), (222) and (420) peaks are missing, because the waves scattered by the second set of FCC atoms cancel them.
