Diffusion in Solids
3D Classroom

Atoms in a solid are not frozen in place. Every second each one tries to jump about 1013 times, and once in a while it makes it over the energy barrier to the next site. That rare success, repeated across a whole crystal, is diffusion. It hardens gear teeth, sets the doping profile of every transistor and opens voids in solder joints. This chapter follows it from one jump to the equations that predict a whole concentration profile.

01

Energy barrier: why heat speeds everything up

fraction above E* = \(e^{-E^*/kT}\)

Reactions in a solid run toward a lower energy, from reactants at Er to products at Ep. They cannot get there directly: on the way the atoms must pass through a crowded, high-energy arrangement, the activated complex M*, at energy E*. The climb ΔE* is the activation energy.

Where does the energy for the climb come from? From heat. At temperature T the atoms share energy according to the Boltzmann distribution: the probability of having energy E is \(\frac{1}{kT}\,e^{-E/kT}\), so most atoms sit low and only a thin tail reaches high. Integrating that tail from E* upward gives the fraction that can cross:

\(\displaystyle \frac{n(E > E^*)}{N_\text{total} } = e^{-E^*/kT}\) \(k = 1.38\times10^{-23}\ \text{J/K} = 8.62\times10^{-5}\ \text{eV/K}\)

The same exponential appeared in Chapter 4 for the vacancy fraction, \(n_v/N = C\,e^{-E_v/kT}\): forming a vacancy is also a climb over an energy cost. Move the sliders. The tail is tiny, but because T sits in the exponent, a modest rise in temperature multiplies it many times.

KEY POINTS
  • A thermally activated process needs a barrier ΔE*. Only the high-energy tail of the Boltzmann distribution gets over it.
  • That fraction is \(e^{-E^*/kT}\). Temperature in the exponent is why the rate changes by orders of magnitude, not by percent.
  • The final energy Ep decides which way the reaction goes; the barrier decides how fast. A product can be much lower in energy and still form very slowly.

02

The Arrhenius plot: a straight line hiding an exponential

rate = \(C\,e^{-Q/RT}\)

The rate of a reaction is proportional to the number of atoms that make it over the barrier, so it carries the same exponential. Written per mole it is the Arrhenius equation:

\(\displaystyle \text{rate} = C\,e^{-Q/RT}\) \(R = k N_A = 8.314\ \text{J/(mol}\cdot\text{K)}\)

Q is the activation energy per mole, and C is a rate constant that does not depend on T. Per particle and per mole are just a unit change: 1 eV per particle = 23.06 kcal/mol = 96.5 kJ/mol.

Take the logarithm and the curve becomes a line, \(\ln(\text{rate}) = \ln C - (Q/R)(1/T)\), or in base 10, \(\log_{10}(\text{rate}) = \log_{10} C - Q/(2.303\,RT)\). Plot log(rate) against 1/T: the intercept is log C and the slope is \(-Q/(2.303\,R)\). That is how activation energies are measured. Measure the rate at a few temperatures, draw the best line, read Q off the slope.

  • log(rate) against 1/T is a straight line. Its slope is \(-Q/(2.303\,R)\), so a steeper line means a higher barrier.
  • A high-Q process is the most sensitive to temperature: the same 100 K gives it a much larger speed-up.
  • Hot is on the left of an Arrhenius plot, because 1/T is small there. Read the axis before reading the line.

03

How atoms jump: vacancies and interstitials

\(E^* = E_v + E_m\) · \(E^* = E_m\)

Diffusion is the transport of one material through another. Smoke spreads through air in seconds, dye through water in minutes; in a solid the same thing takes hours to years, because every step needs a thermally activated jump. There are two main ways to make that jump.

Vacancy (substitutional) diffusion
An atom on a lattice site can move only if a neighboring site is empty. It swaps places with the vacancy, so atoms and vacancies travel in opposite directions. When the atoms are of the host itself this is self-diffusion. Its activation energy has two parts: the energy to form the vacancy and the energy to move into it, \(E^* = E_v + E_m\).
Interstitial diffusion
A small atom sitting in the gaps between host atoms hops from one gap to the next. Almost every neighboring gap is already empty, so no vacancy has to form: \(E^* = E_m\). Carbon in BCC or FCC iron, hydrogen in metals.

Both give a diffusion coefficient of Arrhenius form, \(D = D_0\,e^{-E^*/kT}\). Because interstitial jumps skip the vacancy-formation cost, interstitial diffusion is usually far faster. Watch the two in the model: the red atom is the one jumping, the purple trail is the path of the vacancy or of the carbon atom.

The bonds that hold a crystal together also resist every jump. A metal that needs more heat to melt needs more energy to form and move a vacancy, so the self-diffusion activation energy rises with the melting point. KEY POINTS
  • Vacancy diffusion: an atom swaps with a neighboring vacancy, so atoms and vacancies move in opposite directions. \(E^* = E_v + E_m\).
  • Interstitial diffusion: a small atom hops between gaps. No vacancy is needed, \(E^* = E_m\), so it is usually much faster.
  • The self-diffusion activation energy scales with the melting point: the same bonds resist melting and jumping.

04

The Kirkendall effect: when one side runs faster

\(J_\text{Zn} > J_\text{Cu}\) ⇒ net vacancy flow, moving markers, voids

Press copper against zinc and anneal. If both kinds of atoms crossed the interface at the same rate, the interface would just blur. In 1947 Smigelskas and Kirkendall put fine molybdenum wires at the interface of such a couple as markers, and found that the wires moved. Zinc diffuses faster than copper, so more atoms leave the zinc side than arrive. Every atom that leaves by a vacancy jump sends a vacancy back the other way, so a net flow of vacancies, Jv, pours into the zinc side.

Three things follow. The lattice planes on the zinc side are consumed and those on the copper side are added, so the markers drift toward the zinc. The brass (Cu–Zn) layer grows in the direction the faster-moving zinc flows, into the copper side. And where the arriving vacancies cannot all be absorbed, they cluster into pores, Kirkendall voids. The same voids appear between solder and copper pads (JCu into Sn), where they weaken the joint; chemists use them on purpose to make hollow nanoparticles.

KEY POINTS
  • In a couple, each species has its own diffusivity. Unequal atom fluxes leave a net vacancy flux pointing toward the faster species' side.
  • Markers at the interface drift toward the faster-diffusing side. That drift proved diffusion goes by vacancies, not by atoms swapping directly.
  • Vacancies that pile up form Kirkendall voids, a real reliability problem in solder joints.

05

Fick's first law: steady flow down a gradient

\(J = -D\,dC/dx\)

Each atom jumps in a random direction. None of them "knows" where the low concentration is. Yet where there are more atoms on the left than on the right, more jumps cross any plane from left to right than back, and a net flow appears. The flux J, the net number of atoms crossing unit area per unit time, is proportional to the concentration gradient:

\(\displaystyle J = -D\,\frac{dC}{dx}\)

D is the diffusion coefficient or diffusivity, in m²/s. The minus sign says atoms flow downhill, from high to low concentration. If the two ends are held at fixed concentrations C1 and C2 and nothing reacts, the profile settles into a straight line and stops changing: steady-state diffusion, with the same J through every plane. Purifying hydrogen through a palladium sheet works this way.

The model is a real random walk. Every orange atom takes random steps; the left end is kept full and the right end nearly empty. Count the atoms crossing the purple plane: the net count tracks −D·dC/dx.

5-1One law, three kinds of transport

Flux equals minus a coefficient times a gradient. Heat follows the same rule (Fourier) and so does charge (Ohm). Combine each with conservation and you get the same second-order equation, with its own diffusivity.

MassFick: \(J_M = -D_M\,\frac{dC}{dx}\)concentration gradient\(\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = D_M\,\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial x^2}\)\(D_M\)
HeatFourier: \(J_Q = -k\,\frac{dT}{dx}\)temperature gradient\(\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \frac{k}{\rho c_p}\,\frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x^2}\)\(D_Q = k/\rho c_p\)

KEY POINTS
  • Net flux comes from random jumps plus unequal numbers on the two sides. No force pushes the atoms.
  • J = −D dC/dx: double the gradient or double D and the flux doubles. The minus sign means flow from high to low.
  • Steady state means C(x) no longer changes with time; the profile is a straight line and J is the same everywhere.

06

Fick's second law: how a profile changes with time

\(\partial C/\partial t = D\,\partial^2 C/\partial x^2\)

Most real diffusion is not steady: the concentration at a given depth keeps changing. Take a thin slice between x and x + Δx. Atoms flow in through the left face at J(x) and out through the right face at J(x + Δx). Whatever does not leave stays, so the concentration in the slice changes at

\(\displaystyle \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} = \frac{J(x) - J(x+\Delta x)}{\Delta x} = -\frac{\partial J}{\partial x}\)

This is the continuity equation. Put Fick's first law in for J and, with D constant, you get Fick's second law, \(\partial C/\partial t = D\,\partial^2 C/\partial x^2\). You can read it straight off the curve: the concentration rises where the curve bends upward (\(\partial^2 C/\partial x^2 > 0\), more flows in than out) and falls where it bends downward. Diffusion always flattens bumps.

Two solutions from the slides, both for a Ni–Cu couple at 400 °C, where D of Cu in Ni = \(D_0\,e^{-E_A/kT} = 6\times10^{-8}\ \text{cm}^2\text{/s} \times e^{-0.98\ \text{eV}/kT} = 2.7\times10^{-15}\ \text{cm}^2\text{/s}\):

Couple
Pure Ni joined to Cu0.6Ni0.4: \(C(x,t) = \frac{C_0}{2}\Big[1 - \operatorname{erf}\Big(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt} }\Big)\Big]\). Every profile passes through \(C_0/2\) at the interface.
Thin layer

KEY POINTS
  • Continuity: what flows into a slice and does not flow out accumulates, \(\partial C/\partial t = -\partial J/\partial x\).
  • Fick's second law, \(\partial C/\partial t = D\,\partial^2 C/\partial x^2\): concentration rises where the profile curves up and falls where it curves down.
  • The width of every profile grows as \(\sqrt{Dt}\). Four times the time gives only twice the depth.

07

Carburizing a gear: the error-function solution

\((C_s - C_x)/(C_s - C_0) = \operatorname{erf}(x/2\sqrt{Dt})\)

A steel gear needs a hard, wear-resistant surface and a tough core. Carburizing gets both: heat a low-carbon steel part in a carbon-rich gas, and carbon atoms diffuse in interstitially, building a high-carbon case over the low-carbon core. When the surface is held at Cs and the solid starts at a uniform C0, Fick's second law gives

\(\displaystyle \frac{C_s - C_x}{C_s - C_0} = \operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt} }\right)\) \(\displaystyle \operatorname{erf}(z) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi} }\int_0^z e^{-y^2}\,dy\)

The error function is just a tabulated S-curve: \(\operatorname{erf}(0) = 0\), \(\operatorname{erf}(\infty) = 1\), \(\operatorname{erf}(0.5) = 0.52\). To use the table, find the two rows around your value and interpolate linearly.

Slide example. A 1018 steel gear (0.18 wt% C) is carburized at 927 °C with Cs = 1.15 wt% and D = 1.28 × 10−11 m²/s. How long until the carbon reaches 0.35 wt% at 0.40 mm below the surface? Set the controls to the example and read the answer, then change them to see what matters.

Silicon chips use the same mathematics. Doping a wafer with boron or phosphorus is a constant-surface diffusion (an erf profile) followed by a drive-in from a fixed amount (a Gaussian, Unit 6), and the dopant concentration sets the resistivity of each region.

08

What sets D: temperature, mechanism, structure, path

\(D = D_0\,e^{-Q/RT}\)

The diffusivity depends on five things:

Mechanism
Interstitial atoms (carbon) diffuse far faster than substitutional ones (iron, nickel) in the same host.
Temperature
D rises exponentially with T. This is by far the biggest lever.
Crystal structure
BCC iron packs less densely than FCC iron (APF 0.68 against 0.74), so atoms diffuse faster in it at the same temperature.
Imperfections
Grain boundaries and free surfaces are open, disordered paths. Diffusion along them is much faster than through the lattice.
Concentration
At high solute content D changes: solutes can trap vacancies or block interstitial sites (usually lowering D) or change the activation energy (either way).

The plot draws log D against 1000/T for the systems in Table 5.2 of the slides, each line through its values at 500 °C and 1000 °C. Its slope gives Q. Two-point method from the slides: silver in silver has D = 1 × 10−17 m²/s at 500 °C and 7 × 10−13 m²/s at 1000 °C, so

\(\displaystyle \frac{D_2}{D_1} = \exp\!\left[-\frac{Q}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right)\right]\) ⇒ \(\displaystyle Q = \frac{R\,\ln(7\times10^{4})}{1/773 - 1/1273} \approx 183\ \text{kJ/mol}\)

8-1Fast paths: lattice, grain boundary, surface

What counts as "hot" depends on the melting point. At 100 °C copper is at only 0.275 of its melting temperature and its lattice is practically frozen, but its grain boundaries and surfaces are still active. That is why electromigration in copper wiring runs along surfaces and boundaries. Solder at 100 °C is at 0.82 Tm, so even its lattice diffuses quickly (K. N. Tu, J. Appl. Phys. 94, 5451, 2003).
